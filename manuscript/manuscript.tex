\begin{abstract}


Spatial cross-sectional data encapsulate rich information on spatial processes, serving as a critical foundation for examining causal associations between variables. From a geographical perspective, detecting and quantifying these causal associations is essential for understanding the mechanisms underlying complex natural and human phenomena. However, measuring causal associations from observed spatial cross-sectional data is still a challenge. Although methods such as pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and geographical detector (GD) can and have been widely used to measure the associations between variables in spatial cross-sectional data, these methods fail to effectively quantify the strength of causal associations, as correlation and/or association does not imply causation. Here, we propose a geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) model, which measures the strength of causal associations using the intersectional cardinality (IC) of neighbors identified through mutual cross mapping (MCM) in the reconstructed state space, while integrating the classical DeLong method for ROC curves to test the statistical significance and confidence intervals of causal associations. We validate the accuracy and applicability of GCMC in measuring the strength of causal associations by applying it to three classic spatial cross-sectional datasets with clearly existing causations. Through comparative analysis, we benchmark GCMC against the traditional pearson correlation coefficient, the widely used geographical detector model, and the latest spatial causal model, GCCM, highlighting its effectiveness across both spatial lattice and spatial grid data.


\end{abstract}

\section{Methodology} % or Material and methods

For an observed spatial cross-sectional dataset, GCMC requires three steps to measure the strength of causal associations:

1.  Construct the state spaces: For two spatial variables, \(x\) and \(y\), an appropriate embedding dimension \(E\) and spatial lag step \(\tau\) are required to reconstruct their state spaces \(E_x\) and \(E_y\). In particular, for each spatial unit \(i\), spatial lags of orders \(1, 1+\tau, \dots, 1+(E-1)\tau\) are taken sequentially, and then a statistical aggregation function (typically mean) is applied to aggregate the attribute values of the spatial unit corresponding to different spatial lags into a single vector. Finally, the embedding vectors of all spatial units are stacked row-wise to form the reconstructed state space.

2. Obtain the intersection cardinality (IC) curve: When measuring the causal association from \(y\) to \(x\), for a given number of nearest neighbors \(k\), the number of shared nearest neighbors is counted between the \(k\) nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(k)\) of \(E_y\) and the \(k\) projected nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(k))\) mapped from \(E_x\) to \(E_y\). The IC curve is then obtained by calculating the number of shared nearest neighbors for each value of \(k = 1, 2, \dots, n\). Conversely, the process is also applicable (causal association from \(x\) to \(y\)).

3. Quantify the strength of causal association: The AUC of the different \( k \)-corresponding IC curves obtained in the previous step is calculated to quantify the causal association. Taking the null hypothesis of no causal association between the two variables \(x\) and \(y\), the DeLong method is used to test the statistical significance and confidence interval of the estimated causal association strength.
