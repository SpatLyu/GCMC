\begin{abstract}


Spatial cross-sectional data encapsulate rich information on spatial processes, serving as a critical foundation for examining causal associations between variables. From a geographical perspective, detecting and quantifying these causal associations is essential for understanding the mechanisms underlying complex natural and human phenomena. However, measuring causal associations from observed spatial cross-sectional data is still a challenge. Although methods such as pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and geographical detector (GD) can and have been widely used to measure the associations between variables in spatial cross-sectional data, these methods fail to effectively quantify the strength of causal associations, as correlation and/or association does not imply causation. Here, we propose a geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) model, which measures the strength of causal associations using the intersectional cardinality (IC) of neighbors identified through mutual cross mapping (MCM) in the reconstructed state space, while integrating the classical DeLong method for ROC curves to test the statistical significance and confidence intervals of causal associations. We validate the accuracy and applicability of GCMC in measuring the strength of causal associations by applying it to three classic spatial cross-sectional datasets with clearly existing causations. Through comparative analysis, we benchmark GCMC against the traditional pearson correlation coefficient, the widely used geographical detector model, and the latest spatial causation model, GCCM, highlighting its effectiveness across both spatial lattice and spatial grid data.


\end{abstract}

\section{Methodology} % or Material and methods

For an observed spatial cross-sectional dataset, GCMC requires three steps to measure the strength of causal associations:

1. Construct the state space: For two spatial variables, \(x\) and \(y\), an appropriate embedding dimension \(E\) and spatial lag step \(\tau\) are required to reconstruct their state spaces \(E_x\) and \(E_y\). In particular, for each spatial unit \(i\), spatial lags of orders \(1, 1+\tau, \dots, 1+(E-1)\tau\) are taken sequentially, and then a statistical aggregation function (typically mean) is applied to aggregate the attribute values of the spatial unit corresponding to different spatial lags into a single vector. Finally, the embedding vectors of all spatial units are stacked row-wise to form the reconstructed state space.

2. Obtain the intersection cardinality (IC) curve: When measuring the causal association from \(y\) to \(x\), for a given number of nearest neighbors \(k\), the number of shared nearest neighbors is counted between the \(k\) nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(k)\) of \(E_y\) and the "projected" nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(k))\) mapped from \(E_x\) to \(E_y\). The IC curve is then obtained by calculating the number of shared nearest neighbors for each value of \(k = 1, 2, \dots, n\). Conversely, the process is also applicable (causal association from \(x\) to \(y\)).

3. Quantify the strength of causal association: The AUC of the IC curves derived in the previous step is calculated to quantify the causal association. Taking the null hypothesis of no causal association between the two variables \(x\) and \(y\), with the alternative hypothesis that a causal association exists, the DeLong method is utilized to estimate the AUC values of the IC curves under both hypotheses. Furthermore, the statistical significance and confidence interval of the estimated strength of causal association are also tested to ensure the feasibility of causal inference.

The specific calculation methods for the corresponding steps are shown as follows.

\subsection{State Space Reconstruction (SSR)}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{./figure/figure1.jpg}
\caption{Demonstration of reconstructing state space for spatial cross-sectional data}
\label{fig_ssr}
\end{figure}

According to the Takens-Ma{\~n}{\'e} theorem \citep{Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding}, similar to the approach of \cite{gao2023gccm}, the state space of a spatial cross-sectional variable can be reconstructed using the following formula:

\begin{equation}\label{eq-embedding}
E_x = \begin{bmatrix}
h_{s(1)}(x_1) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_1) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_1) \\
h_{s(1)}(x_2) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_2) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_2) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
h_{s(1)}(x_n) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_n) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_n)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where \(E_x\) denotes the reconstructed state-space matrix for the spatial variable \(x\), each row corresponds to a spatial unit \(x_i\) (\(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\)), and each column represents the embedding of \(x_i\) under progressively higher-order spatial lags. The parameter \(E\) defines the embedding dimension, \(\tau\) specifies the spatial lag step, and \(n\) is the total number of spatial units. The term \(h_{s(1+k\tau)}(x_i)\) represents the spatially lagged transformation of \(x_i\) at the \((1+k\tau)\)-th spatial lag (\(k = 0, 1, \ldots, E-1\)), where \(s(\cdot)\) denotes the spatial lag operator, identifying the neighbors at a specific lag order, and \(h(\cdot)\) is a transformation function that calculates the average attribute value of the neighbors identified by \(s(\cdot)\) at each spatial lag. For instance, as illustrated in figure~\ref{fig_ssr}, when the embedding dimension \(E\) is specified as 3 and the spatial lag step \(\tau\) is 1, the embedding vector for the focal unit \(i\) is computed as \(h_{s(1)}(x_i) = \frac{1145 + 749 + 749 + 972 + 625}{5} = 848\), \(h_{s(2)}(x_i) = \frac{692 + 688 + 479 + 387 + 430 + 885}{6} = 593.5\), and \(h_{s(3)}(x_i) = \frac{734 + 698 + 859 + 323 + 225 + 357}{6} \approx 532.67\), where the denominators correspond to the number of units included in each spatial lag calculation. The numerators represent the sum of the attribute values of the neighbors at the corresponding lag order.

\subsection{Intersectional Cardinality (IC)}

When conducting mutual cross mapping in a reconstructed state space, both linear and nonlinear measures can be applied to quantify the strength of causal association. A prominent example that employs linear method is convergent cross mapping (CCM), which employs a nearest-neighbor weighted sum (simplex projection) and the pearson correlation coefficient to assess causal strength \citep{sugihara1990simplex,sugihara2012ccm}. When mapping variable \(x\) to variable \(y\), it uses the distance of the nearest portion in the state-space vector (embeddings) of \(x\) corresponding to each sample of \(y\) as weights.
These weights are combined with the values of \(y\) at the nearest portions to compute the weighted sum, yielding an estimate \(\widehat{y}\). The pearson correlation coefficient between \(y\) and \(\widehat{y}\) is then calculated to quantify the strength of causal association. The fundamental operations in this process, including the sample-weighted sum and pearson correlation coefficient, are inherently linear manner. On the other hand, the representative method employing a nonlinear approach to quantify the strength of causal association is cross mapping cardinality (CMC). CMC introduces a new causation concept, IC continuity, based on the intersection cardinality of nearest-neighbor sets \citep{tao2023cmc}. By varying the number of nearest neighbors and evaluating the intersection cardinality of the respective nearest-neighbor sets, an IC curve is constructed (analogous to a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve). The area under this curve (AUC value) serves as a measure of causal strength (the IC continuity). The strategy followed by geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) is identical.

Consider two spatial variables \(x\) and \(y\), with their reconstructed state spaces denoted as \(E_x\) and \(E_y\), respectively. When measuring the causal strength from \(y\) to \(x\) using \(k\) nearest neighbors, the corresponding intersectional cardinality for single spatial unit is defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_ik}
IC_{i,k} = \left| E_y^{nn}(i,k) \cap E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\right|
\end{equation}

where \(i\) denotes the \(i\)-th spatial unit in the spatial variables \(x\) and \(y\), and \(k\) represents the number of nearest neighbors. \(E_y^{nn}(i,k)\) refers to the set of \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_y\) corresponding to the \(i\)-th spatial unit, while \(E_x^{nn}(i,k)\) represents the set of \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_x\) corresponding to spatial unit \(i\). The term \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\) represents the union of the \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_y\) corresponding to the spatial units of each nearest neighbor in \(E_x^{nn}(i,k)\). The intersectional cardinality \(IC_{i,k}\) is given by the number of elements in the intersection of \(E_y^{nn}(i,k)\) and \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\).

Each spatial variable typically consists of multiple spatial units. In GCMC, considering all spatial units with a selected number of nearest neighbors \(k\), the intersectional cardinality is given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_k}
IC_k = \frac{\sum_i^n IC_{i,k}}{n}
\end{equation}

By selecting different values of \(k\), the corresponding IC curve can be obtained:

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_curve}
IC_{Curve} = \{IC_{k} | k = 1, 2, \dots, n\}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Causal strength from IC curve}

According to \cite{tao2023cmc}, the strength of causal association can be measured using two contradictory models, \(H_1\) and \(H_0\), as shown in figure~\ref{fig_ic}. When measuring the causal strength from \(y\) to \(x\), the null hypothesis \(H_0\) asserts that there is no causal association from \(y\) to \(x\) (figure~\ref{fig_ic}b), while the alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) posits that a causal association exists from \(y\) to \(x\) (figure~\ref{fig_ic}a):

\begin{equation}\label{eq-hypothesis}
H_0: y \not\Rightarrow x\quad 
H_1: y \Rightarrow x 
\end{equation}

The IC curve corresponding to \(H_1\) can be obtained with equations~\ref{eq-ic_ik},\ref{eq-ic_k},\ref{eq-ic_curve}, while the IC curve corresponding to \(H_0\) can be approximated as a constant curve, \(IC_{Curve} = 0.5k\) \citep{tao2023cmc}. The causal strength (CS) is measured by the AUC values of the IC curves corresponding to \(H_1\) and \(H_0\) (figure~\ref{fig_ic}c):

\begin{equation}\label{eq-cs}
CS_{y \rightarrow x} = AUC(H_1) - AUC(H_0)
\end{equation}
