\begin{abstract}


Spatial cross-sectional data encapsulate rich information on spatial processes, serving as a critical foundation for examining causal associations between variables. From a geographical perspective, detecting and quantifying these causal associations is essential for understanding the mechanisms underlying complex natural and human phenomena. However, measuring causal associations from observed spatial cross-sectional data is still a challenge. Although methods such as pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) and geographical detector (GD) can and have been widely used to measure the associations between variables in spatial cross-sectional data, these methods fail to effectively quantify the strength of causal associations, as correlation and/or association does not imply causation. Here, we propose a geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) model, which measures the strength of causal associations using the intersectional cardinality (IC) of neighbors identified through mutual cross mapping (MCM) in the reconstructed state space, while integrating the classical DeLong method for ROC curves to test the statistical significance and confidence intervals of the causal strength. We validate the accuracy and applicability of GCMC in measuring the strength of causal associations by applying it to three classic spatial cross-sectional datasets with clearly existing causations and benchmark GCMC against the traditional pearson correlation coefficient, the widely used geographical detector model, and the latest spatial causation model, GCCM. The results from the three case datasets indicate that GCMC can effectively measure causal associations in weakly, moderately, and strongly coupled systems while also revealing the dominant causal direction. GCMC is another distinct perspective within empirical dynamic modeling frameworks applied to spatial cross-sectional data and it can be used in conjunction with GCCM to more robustly infer causality from spatial cross-sectional data.


\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Since Galileo’s time, scientific discovery has been driven by both observational analysis and controlled experiments. However, direct interventions in complex systems like the Earth system are often impractical or ethically problematic \citep{gao2022tscess,runge2023tsc,gao2023gccm}. While simulation-based approaches offer a solution, they come with high computational costs and strong underlying assumptions. The increasing availability of large-scale observational datasets, including time series and spatial cross-sectional data, along with advancements in computational power, has opened new avenues for data-driven causal inference in Earth sciences \citep{runge2019esstsc,runge2023tsc}, and a range of causal inference methods based on observational data have been developed, including prediction-based approaches \citep{granger1969grangertest,geweke1982grangertest2,herrera2016spatialgranger}, entropy-based methods \citep{schreiber2000te1,vicente2010te2,sun2014ce1,cafaro2015ce2,sun2015ce3}, structural causal modeling frameworks \citep{pearl195scm1,pearl2000scm2,pearl2009scm3,pearl2018scm4,runge2018scm5,runge2019pcmci}, potential outcome frameworks \citep{rubin1974pom1,rubin1986pom2,rubin2005pom3,delgado2015sdid,kolak2019srdd}, and empirical dynamic modeling frameworks \citep{sugihara2012ccm,ma2014cms,clark2015multispatialccm,leng2020pcm,li2023cme,tao2023cmc,gao2023gccm}.

Although numerous algorithmic models for causal inference based on observational data have been developed, causal inference in Earth system science still faces several challenges. On the one hand, most of these models are designed for time series data, such as PCMCI \citep{runge2019pcmci}, CCM \citep{sugihara2012ccm}, and PCM \citep{leng2020pcm}, and there remains a lack of causal models specifically tailored for spatial cross-sectional data \citep{gao2022ciss,gao2023gccm}. Compared to time series data, spatial cross-sectional data also capture rich information about Earth system processes. \cite{gao2022tscess} suggested that when temporal variations are not prominent, spatial variations can be leveraged to infer causality in Earth systems, highlighting the need to fully utilize spatial cross-sectional data for causal inference. On the other hand, existing models commonly used for causal inference in spatial cross-sectional data, such as PCC \citep{pearson1895pcc}, SAR \citep{anselin2013sar}, GWR \citep{fotheringham2003gwr1,paez2011gwr2}, MGWR \citep{fotheringham2017mgwr}, and GD \citep{wang2010gd1,wang2016gd2,wang2024gd3}, are not strictly designed based on causal principles but are often employed to interpret scenarios with causal implications \citep{gao2022ciss}. In addition, some spatialized event study methods, such as spatial difference in difference \citep{delgado2015sdid} and spatial regression discontinuity designs \citep{kolak2019srdd}, face inherent limitations due to spatial spillover effects and spatial heterogeneity \citep{akbari2021spatialcausality}. The former violates the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA), while the latter fails to identify localized treatment effects \citep{akbari2021spatialcausality,gao2023gccm}, making it difficult to effectively extract causal information from spatial cross-sectional data.

The Earth system is a complex dynamical system with deeply interconnected components \citep{runge2019esstsc,runge2023tsc}. Applying empirical dynamic modeling frameworks, which were originally developed for causal inference in dynamical systems \citep{sugihara2012ccm,leng2020pcm,tao2023cmc}, to spatial cross-sectional data presents a promising approach for inferring causality from a spatial perspective. The recently proposed spatial causation model, geographical convergent cross mapping (GCCM), has demonstrated the potential of applying empirical dynamic modeling frameworks to spatial cross-sectional data \citep{gao2023gccm}. GCCM extends the classical CCM method from the temporal dimension to the spatial dimension \citep{sugihara2012ccm}, pioneering the application of state-space reconstruction techniques \citep{lorenz1969ssr,Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding} from time series data to spatial cross-sectional data. This approach addresses some of the challenges of inferring causality in Earth system science, which existing empirical dynamic modeling frameworks for time series causation models have struggled to resolve \citep{gao2022tscess,gao2023gccm}. Inspired by this, we draw an analogy with cross mapping cardinality (CMC), which utilizes the AUC value of the intersection cardinality(IC) curve to quantify the strength of causal associations \citep{tao2023cmc} and propose a geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) model for measuring the strength of causal associations between variables in spatial cross-sectional data. By combining state-space reconstruction of spatial cross-sectional data and intersection cardinality curve from the reconstructed state space, we innovatively apply the DeLong method \citep{delong1988,sun2014delongfi} to estimate the strength of causal associations reflected by the intersection cardinality curve, along with its statistical significance and confidence intervals. A classic spatial analysis dataset and two case datasets from the GCCM paper were applied to demonstrate the effectiveness of GCMC, while comparing the results with those of PCC, GD, and GCCM to validate the utility of GCMC. In contrast, GCCM struggles to draw conclusions when detecting the causal relationship between elev and tem. We particularly emphasize that GCMC is not intended to replace GCCM, but rather serves as another perspective within empirical dynamic modeling frameworks to examine causal associations. GCMC can complement GCCM, providing a more robust measurement of causal associations from spatial cross-sectional data.

The remainder of this manuscript is structured as follows: Section~\ref{sec2} presents the proposed GCMC method, while Section~\ref{sec3} details its application to the analysis of the selected three datasets. Section~\ref{sec4} discusses the findings and the proposed method, and Section~\ref{sec5} concludes this study.

\section{Geographical Cross Mapping Cardinality (GCMC)}\label{sec2} 

This study introduces the geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) model, designed to quantify the strength of causal associations among variables in spatial cross-sectional data. Given an observed spatial cross-sectional dataset, GCMC requires a three-step sequential process to measure the strength of causal associations:

1. Construct the state space: For two spatial variables, \(x\) and \(y\), an appropriate embedding dimension \(E\) and spatial lag step \(\tau\) are required to reconstruct their state spaces \(E_x\) and \(E_y\). In particular, for each spatial unit \(i\), spatial lags of orders \(1, 1+\tau, \dots, 1+(E-1)\tau\) are taken sequentially, and then a statistical aggregation function (typically mean) is applied to aggregate the attribute values of the spatial unit corresponding to different spatial lags into a single vector. Finally, the embedding vectors of all spatial units are stacked row-wise to form the reconstructed state space.

2. Obtain the intersection cardinality (IC) curve: When measuring the causal association from \(y\) to \(x\), for a given number of nearest neighbors \(k\), the number of shared nearest neighbors is counted between the \(k\) nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(k)\) of \(E_y\) and the "projected" nearest neighbors \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(k))\) mapped from \(E_x\) to \(E_y\). The IC curve is then obtained by calculating the number of shared nearest neighbors for each value of \(k = 1, 2, \dots, n\). Conversely, the process is also applicable (causal association from \(x\) to \(y\)).

3. Quantify the strength of causal association: The AUC of the IC curves derived in the previous step is calculated to quantify the causal association. Taking the null hypothesis of no causal association between the two variables \(x\) and \(y\), with the alternative hypothesis that a causal association exists, the DeLong method is utilized to estimate the AUC values of the IC curves under both hypotheses. Furthermore, the statistical significance and confidence interval of the estimated strength of causal association are also tested to ensure the feasibility of causal inference.

The specific calculation methods for the corresponding steps are shown below.

\subsection{State Space Reconstruction (SSR)}

According to the Takens-Ma{\~n}{\'e} theorem \citep{Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding}, similar to the approach of \cite{gao2023gccm}, the state space of a spatial cross-sectional variable can be reconstructed using the following formula:

\begin{equation}\label{eq-embedding}
E_x = \begin{bmatrix}
h_{s(1)}(x_1) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_1) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_1) \\
h_{s(1)}(x_2) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_2) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_2) \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
h_{s(1)}(x_n) & h_{s(1+\tau)}(x_n) & \cdots & h_{s(1+(E-1)\tau)}(x_n)
\end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where \(E_x\) denotes the reconstructed state-space matrix for the spatial variable \(x\), each row corresponds to a spatial unit \(x_i\) (\(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\)), and each column represents the embedding of \(x_i\) under progressively higher-order spatial lags. The parameter \(E\) defines the embedding dimension, \(\tau\) specifies the spatial lag step, and \(n\) is the total number of spatial units. The term \(h_{s(1+k\tau)}(x_i)\) represents the spatially lagged transformation of \(x_i\) at the \((1+k\tau)\)-th spatial lag (\(k = 0, 1, \ldots, E-1\)), where \(s(\cdot)\) denotes the spatial lag operator, identifying the neighbors at a specific lag order, and \(h(\cdot)\) is a transformation function that calculates the average attribute value of the neighbors identified by \(s(\cdot)\) at each spatial lag. For instance, as illustrated in figure~\ref{fig_ssr}, when the embedding dimension \(E\) is specified as 3 and the spatial lag step \(\tau\) is 1, the embedding vector for the focal unit \(i\) is computed as \(h_{s(1)}(x_i) = \frac{1145 + 749 + 749 + 972 + 625}{5} = 848\), \(h_{s(2)}(x_i) = \frac{692 + 688 + 479 + 387 + 430 + 885}{6} = 593.5\), and \(h_{s(3)}(x_i) = \frac{734 + 698 + 859 + 323 + 225 + 357}{6} \approx 532.67\), where the denominators correspond to the number of units included in each spatial lag calculation. The numerators represent the sum of the attribute values of the neighbors at the corresponding lag order.

\subsection{Intersectional Cardinality (IC)}

When conducting mutual cross mapping in a reconstructed state space, both linear and nonlinear measures can be applied to quantify the strength of causal association. A prominent example that employs linear method is convergent cross mapping (CCM), which employs a nearest-neighbor weighted sum (simplex projection) and the pearson correlation coefficient to assess causal strength \citep{sugihara1990simplex,sugihara2012ccm}. When mapping variable \(x\) to variable \(y\), it uses the distance of the nearest portion in the state-space vector (embeddings) of \(x\) corresponding to each sample of \(y\) as weights.
These weights are combined with the values of \(y\) at the nearest portions to compute the weighted sum, yielding an estimate \(\widehat{y}\). The pearson correlation coefficient between \(y\) and \(\widehat{y}\) is then calculated to quantify the strength of causal association. The fundamental operations in this process, including the sample-weighted sum and pearson correlation coefficient, are inherently linear manner. On the other hand, the representative method employing a nonlinear approach to quantify the strength of causal association is cross mapping cardinality (CMC). CMC introduces a new causation concept based on the intersection cardinality of nearest-neighbor sets \citep{tao2023cmc}. By varying the number of nearest neighbors and evaluating the intersection cardinality of the respective nearest-neighbor sets, an IC curve is constructed (analogous to a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve). The area under this curve (AUC value) serves as a measure of causal strength (the IC continuity). The strategy followed by geographical cross mapping cardinality (GCMC) is identical.

Consider two spatial variables \(x\) and \(y\), with their reconstructed state spaces denoted as \(E_x\) and \(E_y\), respectively. When measuring the causal strength from \(y\) to \(x\) using \(k\) nearest neighbors, the corresponding intersectional cardinality for single spatial unit is defined as

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_ik}
IC_{i,k} = \left| E_y^{nn}(i,k) \cap E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\right|
\end{equation}

where \(i\) denotes the \(i\)-th spatial unit in the spatial variables \(x\) and \(y\), and \(k\) represents the number of nearest neighbors. \(E_y^{nn}(i,k)\) refers to the set of \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_y\) corresponding to the \(i\)-th spatial unit, while \(E_x^{nn}(i,k)\) represents the set of \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_x\) corresponding to spatial unit \(i\). The term \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\) represents the union of the \(k\) nearest neighbors of \(E_y\) corresponding to the spatial units of each nearest neighbor in \(E_x^{nn}(i,k)\). The intersectional cardinality \(IC_{i,k}\) is given by the number of elements in the intersection of \(E_y^{nn}(i,k)\) and \(E_y^{nn}(E_x^{nn}(i,k),k)\).

Each spatial variable typically consists of multiple spatial units. In GCMC, considering all spatial units with a selected number of nearest neighbors \(k\), the intersectional cardinality is given by

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_k}
IC_k = \frac{\sum_i^n IC_{i,k}}{n}
\end{equation}

By selecting different values of \(k\), the corresponding IC curve can be obtained:

\begin{equation}\label{eq-ic_curve}
IC_{curve} = \{IC_{k} | k = 1, 2, \dots, n\}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Causal strength from IC curve}

According to \cite{tao2023cmc}, the strength of causal association can be measured using two contradictory models, \(H_1\) and \(H_0\), as shown in figure~\ref{fig_ic}. When measuring the causal strength from \(y\) to \(x\), the null hypothesis \(H_0\) asserts that there is no causal association from \(y\) to \(x\) (figure~\ref{fig_ic}b), while the alternative hypothesis \(H_1\) posits that a causal association exists from \(y\) to \(x\) (figure~\ref{fig_ic}a):

\begin{equation}\label{eq-hypothesis}
H_0: y \not\Rightarrow x\quad 
H_1: y \Rightarrow x 
\end{equation}

The IC curve corresponding to \(H_1\) can be obtained with equations~\ref{eq-ic_ik},\ref{eq-ic_k},\ref{eq-ic_curve}, while the IC curve corresponding to \(H_0\) can be approximated as a constant curve, \(IC_{curve} = 0.5k\) \citep{tao2023cmc}. The causal strength (CS) is measured by the AUC values of the IC curve under \(H_1\) relative to the IC curve under \(H_0\), which serves as a baseline reference.

\begin{equation}\label{eq-cs}
CS_{y \rightarrow x} = AUC(H_1|H_0) = AUC(H_1) - AUC(H_0)
\end{equation}

In GCMC, The causal strength (AUC value) is estimated using the DeLong method, which is a non-parametric approach for calculating the AUC and its variance \citep{delong1988,sun2014delongfi}. Given a set of positive cases \(\{X_i\}_{i=1}^{m}\) (IC curves corresponding to \(H_1\)) and negative controls \(\{Y_j\}_{j=1}^{n}\) (IC curves corresponding to \(H_0\)), the AUC is computed as:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\theta} = \frac{1}{mn} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \mathbb{I}(X_i > Y_j) + \frac{1}{2} \mathbb{I}(X_i = Y_j),
\end{equation}

where \(\mathbb{I}(\cdot)\) is an indicator function that equals 1 if the condition holds and 0 otherwise.  

To assess the statistical significance and confidence interval of the estimated AUC, the DeLong method provides a variance estimate based on the placement values \citep{delong1988,sun2014delongfi}:

\begin{equation}
\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} \left( X_i - \hat{\theta} \right)^2 + \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \left( Y_j - (1 - \hat{\theta}) \right)^2.
\end{equation}

Using this variance, a confidence interval for AUC can be constructed based on the normal approximation:

\begin{equation}
\text{CI} = \left[ \hat{\theta} - z_{\alpha/2} \hat{\sigma}, \hat{\theta} + z_{\alpha/2} \hat{\sigma} \right],
\end{equation}

where \( z_{\alpha/2} \) is the critical value from the standard normal distribution for a given confidence level (e.g., 1.96 for 95\% confidence). 

To evaluate the statistical significance of \(\hat{\theta}\), a hypothesis test is conducted to determine whether the AUC significantly deviates from 0.5 under the null hypothesis \(\theta = 0.5\), where the test statistic is given by

\begin{equation}
Z = \frac{\hat{\theta} - 0.5}{\hat{\sigma}}.
\end{equation}

The corresponding two-tailed p-value is obtained using the standard normal cumulative distribution function:

\begin{equation}
p = 2 \Phi(-|Z|),
\end{equation}

where \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution.

\section{Results}\label{sec3} 

We employ three real-world datasets to demonstrate the effectiveness of GCMC and compare its results with PCC, GD, and GCCM. The first dataset is the classical Columbus OH spatial analysis dataset \citep{r-spData}, from which we select \textit{hoval} (housing value), \textit{inc} (household income), and \textit{crime} (residential burglaries and vehicle thefts per thousand households in the neighborhood) to investigate their causal relationships. The second and third datasets are derived from two case studies presented in the GCCM paper \citep{gao2023gccm}. The second dataset consists of county-level population density data in mainland China, where we examine the causal interactions among \textit{popd} (population density), \textit{elev} (elevation), and \textit{tem} (temperature). The third dataset involves the farmland NPP dataset in mainland China, analyzing the causal relationships among \textit{npp} (net primary productivity), \textit{pre} (precipitation), and \textit{tem} (temperature). In all three case studies, GCMC effectively quantifies the asymmetric causal strength and accurately identifies the dominant causal direction. The quantitative results align with known prior knowledge, thereby overcoming the limitations of PCC and GD while being consistent with GCCM.

\subsection{Causal associations among housing value, household income, and neighborhood burglary rate}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{./figure/figure3.jpg}
\caption{Causations among hoval, inc and crime}
\label{fig_case1}
\end{figure}

In this case, the variables crime, hoval, and inc from the Columbus OH spatial analysis dataset (as shown in figure~\ref{fig_case1}a–c) are analyzed to investigate their causal associations. These variables exhibit significant correlations (figure~\ref{fig_case1}d), while the strengths of causal associations identified by GCMC and GCCM maintain a consistent hierarchical structure in terms of primary and secondary associations between variable pairs, although numerical differences exist (figure~\ref{fig_case1}f-g). These discrepancies arise from the distinct strategies employed by GCMC and GCCM in quantifying causal associations. It is important to note that, similar to GCCM, GCMC effectively identifies the dominant causal direction (the direction corresponding to the stronger causal association in a given variable pair) within bivariate relationships. However, for secondary causal associations, the identified causal association may either indicate a reversed causal effect (feedback causal influence) or serve as a reflection of the primary causal association \citep{sugihara2012ccm,gao2023gccm}, necessitating further validation. Based on the strengths of causal associations  measured by GCMC in figure~\ref{fig_case1}g, combined with empirical knowledge of the dataset, we constructed a conceptual diagram illustrating the causal associations among crime, hoval, and inc, as shown in figure~\ref{fig_case1}h.

A bidirectional causal association is observed between the variables inc and crime, where the predominant causal direction is from crime to inc (cs = 0.123, p < 0.001). In addition, a weaker reverse causal effect is identified, where inc influences crime (cs = 0.11, p < 0.001). The reverse causality (inc \(\rightarrow\) crime) is that individuals from low-income households may resort to theft or other criminal activities as a means of livelihood. Conversely, the primary causal direction (crime \(\rightarrow\) inc) aligns with the notion that higher crime rates, associated with poor public security, could contribute to reduced investment, commercial activity, and overall economic development, thereby affecting household income levels in those areas. The relationship between the variables inc and hoval is essentially a unidirectional causation, where inc influences hoval (cs = 0.256, p < 0.01), while the effect of hoval on inc is more reflective in nature. Higher household income is generally associated with the purchase of more expensive properties, leading to higher housing values. Conversely, housing value serves as an indicator of household income levels rather than exerting a direct causal influence. There is also a unidirectional causal association between hoval and crime (crime \(\rightarrow\) hoval, cs = 0.247, p < 0.01). Areas with high crime rates, such as theft, directly affect local housing values, whereas high housing values often indicate better local security conditions and fewer instances of crimes like theft. In this case, the effect of hoval on crime is more of a reflection. In contrast, the PD values detected by the GD model may lead to misunderstandings in causation interpretation. The PD values between hoval and crime, as well as between inc and hoval, are merely a measure of the similarity in their spatial distributions. They can serve as a representation of their spatial association but cannot reflect a reasonable causal association. 

\subsection{Causal associations among elevation, temperature, and population density}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{./figure/figure4.jpg}
\caption{Causations among elev, tem and popd}
\label{fig_case2}
\end{figure}

Except for the previous case, we selected a case from the GCCM paper where the correlation between variables( population density, precipitation, and temperature at the county level in mainland China) is weaker compared to the previous case, to test the applicability of GCMC. The results are shown in figure~\ref{fig_case2}. Both the variable elev and the variable tem have causal influence on popd. The causal association from elev to popd is 0.836 (p < 0.001), indicating a strong causation, while tem influences popd with a causal strength of 0.754 (p < 0.001). On the other hand, popd exhibits a feedback causal influence on tem, with a strength of 0.198 (p < 0.001), whereas its relationship with elev is more reflective rather than causative. At the same time, GCMC successfully identified the well-known causation between elevation and temperature (the variable elev and tem), revealing that elevation influences temperature with a strength of 0.628 (p < 0.001). In contrast, the causal influence of temperature on elevation was not statistically significant (p = 0.77), further indicating that temperature partially reflects variations in elevation rather than driving changes in it. This result also demonstrates that the statistical test for causal strength introduced in the GCMC model is effective. Compared to the previous case, the greater numerical difference between the primary and secondary causal strengths in the GCMC measure for this case makes it easier to distinguish whether the secondary causal strength is reverse causation or a reflective relationship. In this context, the statistical significance of the causal strength measured by GCMC can serve as an effective auxiliary criterion. However, it is still necessary to determine whether the significant secondary causal strength represents reverse(feedback) causation or a reflective relationship. For the two pairs of weakly correlated variables (elev and popd, tem and popd), the GD model detects only minimal spatial associations (close to zero). However, there is an actual causation between them. This reinforces the notion that 'weak correlation does not imply no causation', a point that has already been confirmed \citep{pearl2000scm2}.

\subsection{Causal associations among precipitation, temperature, and NPP}

For the well-known NPP-Climate causation, where classical time series causal models failed to capture the causal associations \citep{gao2022tscess,gao2023gccm}, we also tested whether GCMC can effectively measure its causal associations. The relevant results are presented in figure~\ref{fig_case3} and the true causal associations of precipitation and temperature on NPP were detected by GCMC, with a causal strength of 0.609 (p < 0.001) from precipitation to NPP (pre  \(\rightarrow\) npp) and 0.64 (p < 0.001) from temperature to NPP (tem  \(\rightarrow\) npp). The causal association from NPP to precipitation (npp \(\rightarrow\) pre) did not pass the significance test (p = 0.17), indicating that NPP does not exert a causal influence on precipitation. Instead, this suggests that the observed association by GCMC is a result of the enslaved effect of the strong causal influence of precipitation on NPP. In this case, GCMC successfully identified the actual causal associations, whereas the GD model identifies spatial associations where NPP shows a stronger linkage to Pre and Tem than the reverse. This suggests that the strengths of spatial associations derived from the GD model do not provide a reliable indication of the true causal direction. The results of this case indicate that under strong coupling, GCMC can effectively measure the dominant causal associations, while also providing support for diagnosing secondary causal associations. In this case, the data is organized in a spatial grid format, whereas the previous two cases use spatial lattice data, thereby demonstrating that GCMC is applicable not only to spatial lattice data but also to spatial grid data.
