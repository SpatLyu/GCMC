\begin{reply}
We thank the editor for the opportunity to revise our manuscript. In light of the editor’s and reviewers’ constructive suggestions, we have substantially revised the manuscript to improve its clarity, methodological rigor, and overall quality. Below, we summarize the major revisions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item In the revised manuscript, we have clarified the applicability of the proposed GCMC method (Lines 440--446). As GCMC is grounded in dynamical systems theory, it is expected to be most effective when applied to spatial cross-sectional data generated by deterministic (i.e., non-purely random) processes. When spatial patterns are completely random or when observational data are dominated by stochastic noise, GCMC may fail to provide reliable causal inference. In addition, we have clarified the central problem addressed in this study: existing causal discovery methods for spatial cross-sectional data often exhibit relatively high false positive rates, whereas the proposed GCMC tends to achieve lower false positive causal identification. This is supported by comparative results from both benchmark simulations and real-world case studies.
  
  \item We have also provided empirical guidelines in the revised manuscript for selecting three key parameters when applying GCMC to measure causal strength. Specifically, the embedding dimension $E$ can be determined using the false nearest neighbors method. The number of nearest neighbors $k$ used in constructing the IC curve for causal strength evaluation can be empirically set to the ceiling of the square root of the product of the sample size involved in cross mapping and the embedding dimension $E$ (Lines 407--419). For causal strength inference, an empirical threshold of 0.2 can be adopted, while statistical significance can be assessed using the conventional critical value of 0.05. Both the causal strength threshold and the significance level may be relaxed or made more stringent depending on the application context. In all three case studies, GCMC was applied using this empirical parameter selection strategy and demonstrated stable and reasonable performance.
  
  \item In response to the suggestions from the editor and the two reviewers, we have added a graphical justification for the IC calculation and included LiNGAM as a comparative causal method in all three case studies. In addition, based on the newly established empirical parameter selection strategy for the three key GCMC parameters, we reran the case studies and the noise sensitivity analysis, and accordingly revised the \textit{Material and methods}, \textit{Results}, and \textit{Discussion} sections. We also refined the \textit{Abstract} and \textit{Introduction} to clearly articulate the research gap and the specific research question addressed in this study.

\end{itemize}

We hope that this extensive revisions address the reviewers’ concerns, and we respectfully submit the revised manuscript for review.

\end{reply}

\reviewersection

\begin{point}
The main methodological problem that I see with this method is its main idea to infer causality from consistency of spatial patterns between spatial variables. The original idea, temporal delay embedding (Takens, 1981) leverages the fact that time introduces a directed dependence—past states determine future evolution—so if variable x can reconstruct the manifold of y through time delays, it reflects causality encoded in temporal successions. Spatial embeddings, in contrast, lack such directionality: spatial coordinates capture coexistence rather than succession. consistency between spatial embeddings of x and y however only indicates similarity between spatial autocorrelation, not causal influence. Without an explicit process model describing how effects propagate across space, spatial embedding consistency captures only a necessary, but not a sufficient condition of causality. What is lacking is the control of counterfactual conditions, which only allows to infer causality (Pearl, Rubin, Peters & Schölkopf, etc.). For this reason, I would expect that the method still should be prone to confounding and other causal spurious effects.
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
We sincerely thank the reviewer for this insightful and theoretically grounded critique. The concern centers on two pivotal issues:  
(i) the validity of reconstructing state space from spatial cross-sectional data using spatial lags, and 
(ii) the legitimacy of inferring causality from the intersectional cardinality of cross-mapped neighborhoods between reconstructed shadow manifolds. We attempt to address these issues in turn, with a focused emphasis on clarifying potential conceptual ambiguities that may have contributed to misunderstandings during the review process.

\textbf{On state space reconstruction:}  
Takens' embedding theorem establishes that, given (a) a smooth deterministic dynamical system evolving on a compact $m$-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M}$, and (b) a smooth scalar observable $h:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfying genericity conditions, the delay-coordinate mapping
\[
\Phi(t) = \big< h(z(t)),\, h(z(t - \tau)),\, \dots,\, h(z(t - (d-1)\tau)) \big>
\]
with embedding dimension $d \geq 2m+1$ yields a reconstructed manifold $\tilde{\mathcal{M}}$ that is diffeomorphic to $\mathcal{M}$ \citep{Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding}. This diffeomorphism ensures that the topological and dynamical invariants of the original system, such as Lyapunov exponents, fractal dimensions, and periodic orbits, are preserved in the reconstructed space. Consequently, the intrinsic dynamics of the system can be faithfully analyzed from a single observed variable. Crucially, these geometric guarantees are independent of any causal interpretation of time delays. The theorem relies solely on the structure of the underlying dynamical system and the smoothness of the observation function \citep{sauer1991generalized_embedding_theorem1,wells2017generalized_embedding_theorem2}.

The ``delay'' in Takens' framework is merely a device to generate a set of coordinates that collectively span the system’s state space, thereby enabling reconstruction of its underlying geometry. In spatial settings, where the indexing set consists of geographic locations (spatial units) rather than temporal instants, an analogous embedding can be constructed by replacing temporal lags with spatial neighbors. Specifically, for a spatial field $x(\mathbf{s})$ defined over locations $\mathbf{s} \in \mathcal{S} \subset \mathbb{R}^2$, one defines a spatial embedding map as
\[
\Psi(\mathbf{s}) = \big< x(\mathbf{s}),\, x(\mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\delta}_1),\, x(\mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\delta}_2),\, \dots,\, x(\mathbf{s} + \boldsymbol{\delta}_{d-1}) \big>,
\]
where $\{\boldsymbol{\delta}_i\}_{i=1}^{d-1}$ are displacement vectors specifying neighboring locations within a local spatial neighborhood.

This construction is theoretically justified when the observed spatial cross-sectional data arises as a snapshot of a spatially coupled dynamical system, for instance, a reaction-diffusion process at quasi-steady state, an advection-diffusion equilibrium, or a network of interacting spatial units. In such systems, the state at location $\mathbf{s}$ is dynamically influenced by the states of its neighbors, implying that the joint configuration of $x$ over a local neighborhood encodes information about the local system state. Under the assumptions that (i) the underlying spatial process is generated by a smooth deterministic dynamics on a compact manifold, and (ii) the spatial sampling is sufficiently dense to resolve local interactions, the spatial embedding map $\Psi$ can be viewed as a spatial analogue of the delay-coordinate map. Consequently, the reconstructed shadow manifold $\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{\text{spatial}}$ preserves the geometric structure of the latent spatial dynamics, up to a diffeomorphism. Therefore, spatial embedding is not a heuristic analogy to temporal embedding but a mathematically coherent extension of the same geometric reconstruction principle to spatial cross-sectional data, provided the spatial field exhibits sufficient local variability and coupling structure.

\textbf{On causal inference in GCMC:}  
GCMC does \textit{not} infer causality from the consistency of spatial patterns in the original spatial variables, nor from any direct consistency between their reconstructed shadow manifolds (spatial embeddings). Rather, it infers causation by assessing whether local neighborhoods in \(M_x\) are consistently mapped to corresponding neighborhoods in \(M_y\) (lines~126--141 describe this procedure in detail). This asymmetric neighborhood recoverability reflects the directional dynamical coupling between variables. Such asymmetry can be operationalized either through local weighted prediction as in convergent cross mapping \citep{sugihara1990simplex,sugihara2012ccm}, or as in GCMC, through the intersectional cardinality of projected neighborhoods \citep{tao2023cmc}. The latter is graphically illustrated in the newly added Figure~2 and provides a concrete operationalization of mutual cross mapping.

GCMC is grounded in dynamical systems theory and operationalizes a notion of \textit{dynamical causality}. Specifically, it leverages reconstructed shadow manifolds to assess whether the state of one variable can be recovered from the state space of another \citep{sugihara2012ccm,gao2023gccm,tao2023cmc}. This approach does not rely on counterfactual reasoning, potential outcomes, or explicit intervention models. The counterfactual framework of causality, as referenced by the reviewer, constitutes a distinct inferential paradigm, primarily designed for settings where interventions or randomized assignments are feasible (lines~37--43 discuss these differing conceptualizations of causality). In contrast, empirical dynamic modeling, upon which GCMC is built, is tailored for observational, complex systems where the underlying mechanisms are unknown but the dynamics are sufficiently structured and deterministic. Consequently, GCMC is not intended as a substitute for controlled experiments, but rather as a \textit{causal discovery tool} aimed at reducing false positives and identifying relative causal influence under spatial cross-sectional constraints. Our benchmark simulations explicitly include both confounding and collider structures, and the results show that GCMC is still able to correctly identify the true causal influences under these challenging scenarios (Figure~4 and lines~289--303).

We further clarify that the validity of GCMC relies on a set of explicit assumptions associated with spatial lag embedding and the use of intersectional cardinality for mutual cross mapping. These assumptions, including the presence of sufficient spatial variability and an underlying deterministic or weakly stochastic dynamical structure, are now clearly stated in the revised manuscript (lines~440--446). In addition, we explicitly distinguish the causal strength measured by GCMC from counterfactual causal effects defined under interventionist frameworks (lines~187--199). In particular, the GCMC measure quantifies the strength of dynamical coupling and recoverability between state spaces, rather than the effect of hypothetical interventions. This clarification is intended to delimit the scope of inference supported by GCMC and to avoid conflating dynamical causality with counterfactual causality.

Within this clearly delimited scope, the two methodological components questioned by the reviewer correspond to practically viable solutions that are grounded in prior literature. As stated in the \textit{Conclusion} (lines~459--462), the use of spatial lag embedding in GCMC follows strategies already adopted in previously published spatial causal methods, such as geographical convergent cross mapping and geographical pattern causality \citep{gao2023gccm,zhang2025gpc}. Similarly, the use of intersectional cardinality as a measure of cross-mappability has been operationally implemented in earlier work \citep{tao2023cmc}. These precedents support the methodological coherence of GCMC under the stated assumptions.

We hope that our responses above have helped clarify the methodological foundations of GCMC and address the reviewer’s concerns. We sincerely thank the reviewer once again for raising this insightful point, which has provided us with a valuable opportunity to reflect deeply on the theoretical underpinnings of our work.
\end{reply}

We thank the reviewer for this insightful comment. We agree that broader benchmarking is important for evaluating the effectiveness of a new causal discovery method. In the revised manuscript, we have therefore expanded the comparative evaluation by including LiNGAM as an additional causal discovery baseline. LiNGAM represents a widely used causal inference approach with fundamentally different assumptions from GCMC and GCCM, providing a more stringent and informative point of comparison.

The results of this additional comparison are now reported and discussed in the revised manuscript (Figure~5f, lines~317--318 for the residential crime case study; Figure~6c, lines~326--327 for the population density case study; and Figure~7c, lines~348--349 for the farmland net primary productivity case study), demonstrating that GCMC maintains more robust performance in spatial cross-sectional settings, particularly in reducing false-positive causal detections, relative to LiNGAM. We further observe that LiNGAM often produces spurious or uninterpretable causal links when applied to spatial cross-sectional data, failing to capture meaningful causal relationships and frequently misidentifying causal influences. We believe that this expanded benchmark substantially strengthens the empirical evidence for the proposed method.
\end{reply}

\begin{point}
Furthermore, regarding the practicalities of the method itself, I see the problem that it makes heavy use of three modeling parameters that would need to be set in advance to make sure the method can distinguish causal from non-causal cases: a threshold parameter for effect strengths (in the experiments, this was 0.1, but how to determine this in general?), a significance threshold for the IC curve (which the authors don't seem to talk about), as well as a the spatial neighborhood lag parameter (k). All these parameters need to be set in a meaningful way (using what kind of knowledge?) before we can expect any meaningful outcomes. This shows that having a simple significance test for causality is illusory. In the end, you need a-priori knowledge to set the parameters right. This makes the whole approach a bit questionable I would say, though it might still be practically useful.
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful comment on the practical aspects of parameter selection in GCMC, which gives us an opportunity to clarify and re-examine this important issue. In the revised manuscript, we have explicitly clarified the empirical strategies used to determine the three key parameters involved in GCMC.

First, the embedding dimension \(E\) can be guided by the false nearest neighbours (FNN) method, which is a standard practice in empirical dynamic modeling \citep{kennel1992fnn}. Given the selected embedding dimension \(E\) and the sample size used for cross mapping \(N\), the neighborhood size \(k\) can then be empirically determined using the heuristic rule \(k = \lceil \sqrt{N E} \rceil\), which balances local neighborhood representation and statistical robustness. We discuss the influence of \(k\) and empirical strategies for its selection in the \textit{Discussion} section (lines~407--419).

Second, the statistical significance level for causal strength is set to 0.05, as now explicitly stated in lines~185--186 of the revised manuscript. This significance level is used to assess whether the observed divergence between intersectional cardinality curves under the null and alternative hypotheses is statistically meaningful.

Third, regarding the causal strength threshold, we now recommend an empirical threshold of 0.2. This value has demonstrated stable and interpretable performance across all three case studies presented in the paper, and the rationale for this choice is discussed in detail in lines~200--206 of the revised manuscript. In addition, the noise sensitivity analysis of GCMC examines the conditions under which this reference threshold of 0.2 may break down and discusses the underlying reasons for its failure (lines~364--371).

All case studies in the revised manuscript consistently apply this empirical parameter selection strategy and yield coherent and robust results. While we agree with the reviewer that causal inference inevitably requires informed parameter choices and cannot rely on a single universally optimal significance test, we hope that the clarified parameterization strategy improves the transparency of the proposed GCMC approach and provides practical guidance for its application.
\end{reply}

\begin{point}
Interpretation of the causal strength derived from the IC curve: I believe that these methods do not so much as quantify the treatment effect of a variable $X_1$, as much as enable to place the strength of this effect in context of other variables $X_2, \dots, X_n$. I am unsure whether the causal strength derived from the AUC of the Cardinality curve can be interpreted this way. SO it may be a good mechanism for causal discovery, or even ranking of the treatment effects of multiple variables, but I am not sure how it would relate to claims about absolute effect strength. Can you address the interpretation of the values you get from your GCMC measure's outcomes?
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
We thank the reviewer for this thoughtful and important comment regarding the interpretation of causal strength. We agree that the notion of causal strength in GCMC differs fundamentally from a traditional treatment-effect interpretation. In the revised manuscript (lines~187--199), we have clarified that the causal strength measured by GCMC is rooted in dynamical systems theory and is defined by the divergence between intersectional cardinality curves under the null and alternative hypotheses of mutual cross mapping. As such, it should not be interpreted as a treatment effect, such as the marginal change in an outcome variable induced by a unit change in a predictor while controlling for confounders.

Instead, the GCMC causal strength represents a relative measure of causal influence, reflecting how consistently the state of one variable can be recovered from another across neighborhood scales in the reconstructed state space. From this perspective, GCMC is particularly well suited for causal discovery and for comparing the relative strength of directional causal coupling among multiple candidate drivers. Larger values indicate stronger causal influence in a comparative sense, enabling ranking and contrast across variables, rather than direct claims about absolute effect magnitudes. We have emphasized that this interpretation is consistent with the empirical dynamic modeling framework, where causation is inferred through information recovery rather than parametric effect sizes.
\end{reply}
