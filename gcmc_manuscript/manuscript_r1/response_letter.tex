\begin{reply}
We thank the editor for the opportunity to revise our manuscript. In light of the editor’s and reviewers’ constructive suggestions, we have substantially revised the manuscript to improve its clarity, methodological rigor, and overall quality. Below, we summarize the major revisions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item In the revised manuscript, we have clarified the applicability of the proposed GCMC method (Lines 440--446). As GCMC is grounded in dynamical systems theory, it is expected to be most effective when applied to spatial cross-sectional data generated by deterministic (i.e., non-purely random) processes. When spatial patterns are completely random or when observational data are dominated by stochastic noise, GCMC may fail to provide reliable causal inference. In addition, we have clarified the central problem addressed in this study: existing causal discovery methods for spatial cross-sectional data often exhibit relatively high false positive rates, whereas the proposed GCMC tends to achieve lower false positive causal identification. This is supported by comparative results from both benchmark simulations and real-world case studies.
  
  \item We have also provided empirical guidelines in the revised manuscript for selecting three key parameters when applying GCMC to measure causal strength. Specifically, the embedding dimension $E$ can be determined using the false nearest neighbors method. The number of nearest neighbors $k$ used in constructing the IC curve for causal strength evaluation can be empirically set to the ceiling of the square root of the product of the sample size involved in cross mapping and the embedding dimension $E$ (Lines 407--419). For causal strength inference, an empirical threshold of 0.2 can be adopted, while statistical significance can be assessed using the conventional critical value of 0.05. Both the causal strength threshold and the significance level may be relaxed or made more stringent depending on the application context. In all three case studies, GCMC was applied using this empirical parameter selection strategy and demonstrated stable and reasonable performance.
  
  \item In response to the suggestions from the editor and the two reviewers, we have added a graphical justification for the IC calculation and included LiNGAM as a comparative causal method in all three case studies. In addition, based on the newly established empirical parameter selection strategy for the three key GCMC parameters, we reran the case studies and the noise sensitivity analysis, and accordingly revised the \textit{Material and methods}, \textit{Results}, and \textit{Discussion} sections. We also refined the \textit{Abstract} and \textit{Introduction} to clearly articulate the research gap and the specific research question addressed in this study.

\end{itemize}

We hope that this extensive revisions address the reviewers’ concerns, and we respectfully submit the revised manuscript for review.

\end{reply}

\begin{point}
The main methodological problem that I see with this method is its main idea to infer causality from consistency of spatial patterns between spatial variables. The original idea, temporal delay embedding (Takens, 1981) leverages the fact that time introduces a directed dependence—past states determine future evolution—so if variable x can reconstruct the manifold of y through time delays, it reflects causality encoded in temporal successions. Spatial embeddings, in contrast, lack such directionality: spatial coordinates capture coexistence rather than succession. consistency between spatial embeddings of x and y however only indicates similarity between spatial autocorrelation, not causal influence. Without an explicit process model describing how effects propagate across space, spatial embedding consistency captures only a necessary, but not a sufficient condition of causality. What is lacking is the control of counterfactual conditions, which only allows to infer causality (Pearl, Rubin, Peters & Schölkopf, etc.). For this reason, I would expect that the method still should be prone to confounding and other causal spurious effects.
\end{point}
\begin{reply}
We acknowledge the reviewer’s concern regarding the theoretical basis of GCMC, particularly the adaptation from temporal to spatial domains and the introduction of the Intersectional Cardinality (IC) indicator. 

We would like to clarify that GCMC is grounded in generalized embedding theory \citep{Takens1981embedding,Mane1981embedding}, which posits that the dynamics of a system can be reconstructed from a single observable through delay-coordinate embedding. While traditional CCM employs time-delayed observations to construct state spaces, the GCMC method extends this principle to spatial domains by treating spatial lags as analogs of temporal lags. Although spatial lags differ in that multiple spatially adjacent values exist at a single lag step (as opposed to single temporal observations), this distinction is addressed through appropriate statistical aggregation before embedding. This spatial extension maintains the theoretical foundation established by dynamical systems theory and ensures that the shadow manifolds reconstructed from spatial neighbors can capture the intrinsic structure of the system.

Regarding the IC indicator, its introduction is motivated by the following principle: if two variables are driven by a shared underlying dynamical mechanism, their reconstructed state spaces (or manifolds) will exhibit structural overlap \citep{sugihara2012ccm,gao2023gccm}. This overlap allows for mutual cross mapping, as in CCM, but can also be directly reflected by the similarity in local neighborhood structures \citep{tao2023cmc}. GCMC leverages this idea by quantifying the degree of such structural overlap using the IC metric—i.e., the intersectional cardinality of nearest neighbors across reconstructed manifolds. This provides a compact, interpretable, and directional measure of dynamical causality, rooted in shared system dynamics.

To address the reviewer’s concern more explicitly, we have expanded the discussion of these theoretical underpinnings in Section~2.1 (Lines~87--91 and Lines~102--108) of the revised manuscript.
\end{reply}

\begin{point}
The authors designed evaluation experiments that in principle seems to be defensible (based on simulating spatial variables under different known causal model assumptions), and the results show that the method is able to distinguish some causal from non-causal effects. However, the evidence is very limited and preliminary: the benchmark comparison is only made with respect to their own previously developed method (GCCM) and with respect to non-causal methods like simple Pearson correlations and GD. That alone is not yet very convincing, because it leaves out all other approaches to causality detection that the method could be compared against.
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
We appreciate the reviewer’s careful examination and critical feedback. This comment helped us carefully reassess the logic and methodological rigor behind applying GCMC to real-world dataset.

In the original version of the manuscript, we underestimated the influence of the embedding dimension $E$ on the performance of GCMC. Specifically, we adopted a simple rule-of-thumb—setting $E = 2$ for smaller datasets and $E = 3$ for larger ones—without systematic justification. However, since the core of our method relies on the intersectional structure of nearest neighbors in reconstructed state spaces, using an inadequately low embedding dimension can lead to a high proportion of false nearest neighbors\citep{kennel1992fnn,ma2017cme}, thereby distorting the reconstructed manifold geometry and biasing the estimated causal strength.

To address this critical issue, we have now implemented the \textit{false nearest neighbors} method to determine appropriate embedding dimensions \citep{kennel1992fnn} for all variables prior to running GCMC or GCCM. The updated procedure is described in Lines~216--220 of the revised manuscript.

Furthermore, we have re-run all three case studies and the noise sensitivity analysis using the corrected embedding dimensions. The causal diagrams for the three case studies have been revised accordingly, incorporating both statistical significance testing and a stricter causal strength threshold to determine the presence of links. The updated results (presented in Figures~4–6 of the revised manuscript) no longer exhibit the inconsistencies criticized in the original submission. Specifically, in the first example, only causal links that are both statistically significant and have strength exceeding 0.1 are retained in the causal diagram (i.e., \texttt{crime}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{hoval}, \texttt{inc}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{hoval}; see Figure~\ref{fig_case1} below, or Figure~4 in the revised manuscript). In the second example, the causal link from temperature to elevation is excluded due to lack of statistical significance, while the link from population density to elevation is removed because its strength falls below the 0.10 threshold (see Figure~\ref{fig_case2} below or Figure~5 in the revised manuscript). In the third example, the link from NPP to temperature is excluded as well, owing to its lack of statistical significance (see Figure~\ref{fig_case3} below or Figure~6 in the revised manuscript). Lines~156–169 of the revised manuscript explain how causation is inferred from the causal strengths measured by GCMC.

\begin{point}
Furthermore, regarding the practicalities of the method itself, I see the problem that it makes heavy use of three modeling parameters that would need to be set in advance to make sure the method can distinguish causal from non-causal cases: a threshold parameter for effect strengths (in the experiments, this was 0.1, but how to determine this in general?), a significance threshold for the IC curve (which the authors don't seem to talk about), as well as a the spatial neighborhood lag parameter (k). All these parameters need to be set in a meaningful way (using what kind of knowledge?) before we can expect any meaningful outcomes. This shows that having a simple significance test for causality is illusory. In the end, you need a-priori knowledge to set the parameters right. This makes the whole approach a bit questionable I would say, though it might still be practically useful.
\end{point}

\begin{reply}
Thank you very much for your insightful comments. The main advantage of GCMC compared to GCCM lies in its relatively lower false positive rate and fewer causal omissions. For instance, in our own controlled benchmark tests where the causal structure is known, GCCM either misses causal paths or detects false positives in all three scenarios (as illustrated in Figure~3 of the revised manuscript, or as shown below). 

Regarding the specific case you mentioned in the NPP study, the issue you raised is resolved in GCMC when using the correct embedding dimension \( E \). In the revised results, the causal strength from NPP to temperature is statistically insignificant, leading us to exclude the causal link from NPP to temperature altogether.
